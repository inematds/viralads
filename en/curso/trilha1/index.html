🤝 What UGC is and why it sells more

UGC is an ad that looks like it was made by a real person. That “real person” feel lowers people’s guard and sells much more.

What it is:

UGC means “user-generated content.” In practice, it’s a video of an everyday person talking about a product while looking at their phone’s camera, as if sharing a tip with a friend.

Why learn:

It’s the fastest-growing and highest-converting ad format today. Understanding the concept is the first step to stop making “commercials” and start making “recommendations.”

Key concepts:

Phone, not a studio. Person, not brand. Conversation, not voice-over. It looks organic, but it’s a paid ad.

What it is:

When something "feels like an ad"—stiff, perfect, corporate—the person immediately becomes guarded. There’s an automatic distrust.

Why learn:

All the power of UGC comes from breaking down this barrier. If you understand what switches on the customer’s "skeptical mode," you know what to avoid.

Key concepts:

Too perfect = suspicious. Real people = trustworthy. Trust comes before the sale.

What it is:

Market data shows UGC converting about 10x better, with 300% better click-through, ~50% lower cost per click, and ~73% higher engagement than brand ads.

Why learn:

Numbers take the guesswork out of the decision. You start investing in the format with the best cost-to-results ratio.

Key concepts:

~10x conversion · +300% clicks · −50% cost per click · +73% engagement.

What it is:

In the feed, people decide in 1 second whether to keep watching. If the video screams "ad," their thumb is already scrolling down.

Why learn:

If no one watches, nothing else matters. UGC wins this first battle by looking like just another video in the feed.

Key concepts:

The first 3 seconds decide everything. Looking native to the feed = more people watching.

What it is:

We trust people more than brands. A video featuring an everyday person works like a recommendation from someone you know — "look what I found."

Why learn:

Knowing that you're selling "a recommendation," not "a product," changes the tone of everything you create.

Key concepts:

Social proof. Word of mouth at scale. A friend’s tone, not a salesperson’s.

What it is:

It works for stores, services, digital products, restaurants—anything that depends on people clicking and buying after watching a video.

Why learn:

So you can leave already thinking about which of your products will be your first UGC test.

Key concepts:

Start with the product you most want to sell. One video, one product, one clear call to action.

🧭 The hunter's mindset

The big shift: you don't need to be creative from scratch. You need to be good at finding what's already selling and intelligently copying its framework.

What it is:

It’s extremely rare to get a brand-new ad right on the first try. The smart way is to start with what’s already proven to sell and make your own version.

Why learn:

It saves money and time. You reduce the risk of testing from scratch and start with something validated by the market.

Key concepts:

The wheel already exists. Adapt it, don’t reinvent it. Lower risk, faster results.

What it is:

Three steps: hunt (find what sells), study (understand why), and launch (make your version and test it).

Why learn:

It’s the map for the entire course. Each track ahead is one of these three steps.

Key concepts:

Hunt = Track 2. Study = Track 3. Launch = Track 4.

What it is:

Meta and TikTok’s ad libraries are public and free. Everything your competitors are running is there, out in the open.

Why learn:

You have an endless, free "inspiration library." It’s your main source of validated ideas.

Key concepts:

Public and free. See any brand. Your personal collection of ads that work.

What it is:

You copy the structure what works (the type of hook, the pace, the call to action), not the competitor’s product or images.

Why learn:

It’s the difference between drawing inspiration intelligently and making a cheap copy no one respects.

Key concepts:

Same structure, different content. Same formula, your brand and your product.

What it is:

When a brand finds a winning ad, it makes dozens of small variations of it (blue shirt, red shirt, indoors, outdoors...).

Why learn:

It’s a golden signal: lots of variations of the same ad = that formula is selling. You’ll learn to read this signal.

Key concepts:

Lots of variations = a winner. Small changes, same formula.

What it is:

It’s worth accelerating with ads when something is already showing signs of life: it sells organically or already works in other people’s ads. Don’t force something that’s never been tested.

Why learn:

It keeps you from wasting money pushing a product the market hasn't wanted yet.

Key concepts:

Signal before scaling. Proven organically or through ads. Accelerate what’s already working.

⏳ The ad life cycle

An ad is a living thing: it’s born, some become winners, and most die quickly. Learning to read those signs is what separates the amateur from the hunter.

What it is:

The vast majority of ads stop running within 60 days because they didn't get results and the company cut them.

Why learn:

The ones that last a long time are exactly the ones you want to study. Others' "deaths" reveal the winners.

Key concepts:

Bad ads get cut quickly. Survival = proof that it sells.

What it is:

The longer an ad runs, the more likely it is to be making a profit. 30 days is already a good sign; 60+ is a "unicorn".

Why learn:

The start date appears in the ad library. It's the first filter for separating what’s worth studying.

Key concepts:

≥30 days = legitimate. ≥60 days = rare winner. Check the start date.

What it is:

Impressions are how many times the ad was shown. Many impressions indicate that the company is putting a lot of money into it.

Why learn:

No one invests heavily in an ad that doesn’t deliver. High impressions are a clue that it’s a winner.

Key concepts:

Impressions = times shown. More impressions = more investment = more likely to sell.

What it is:

When you see many nearly identical copies of the same ad running at the same time, the brand has found a formula that works.

Why learn:

It’s one of the most reliable signs. Count the variations: lots of them = confirmed winning formula.

Key concepts:

Lots of variations = a strong bet. Count how many versions there are.

What it is:

When a brand releases 15-20 new ads at once in several places, it's betting big on that idea.

Why learn:

Peaks like these often mark the birth of a winning campaign. They’re worth watching.

Key concepts:

Mass launch = a bet. Multiple channels at once = confidence.

What it is:

After analyzing and running 50 to 100 ads, clear patterns start to emerge — then you scale what works.

Why learn:

It takes the pressure off getting it right on the first try. The game is volume + observation + repeating the winner.

Key concepts:

Volume reveals patterns. Test, observe, scale. Patience with a method.
